Self-educated — method

Rubric v1 · last changed 6 September 2026

How this is scored

Everything this assessment does to your answers, in the order it does it, including the parts that are opinion and the parts it gets wrong.

The short version

Eighteen questions measure what you like doing. Twelve measure what you have actually made. A written rubric describes what each of seventeen fields asks of people entering it. Your knowledge is compared against that rubric, your interests against the field's interest profile, and the two are combined 55/45 in favour of knowledge. Every resulting number is reported with the margin of error the underlying measurement supports — which is wider than most tests of this kind admit.

The interest questions

These are a RIASEC inventory — the six-type model (Realistic, Investigative, Artistic, Social, Enterprising, Conventional) proposed by John Holland in 1959 and used by the US Department of Labor's O*NET database to describe every occupation it lists. It is used here so results can eventually be joined to real occupation data rather than to our opinion.

This is a screener, not the instrument. O*NET's own short-form Interest Profiler uses sixty items; the full version uses one hundred and eighty. This uses eighteen, three per type, because nobody finishes a sixty-item form they did not come for. Three items per scale caps internal consistency at roughly α = .73, against .90 or better for the real thing. That is the single biggest limitation here, and it is why the results carry a margin.

Two guards run on your answers. Your six type scores are centred on your own mean before being matched, so answering "strongly like" to everything scores no better than answering "slightly" to everything — only the shape of your preferences counts, not its height. And a one-way ANOVA across the six scales checks whether your profile has any shape at all; if it does not, the interest term is dropped entirely and the page tells you so rather than inventing a match.

The twelve domain ratings

These are yours, and nobody has checked them. They are asked behaviourally — what you have made, who used it, who paid for it — because that is the only kind of self-rating people answer with any accuracy. "How good are you at X" measures confidence; "what have you built in X" measures something closer to the truth.

Nothing adjusts these upward. An earlier version of this tool quietly added up to a third of a level based on what you said you enjoyed. That has been removed: if enjoyment were evidence of skill, this whole product would be unnecessary. Where your answers contradict each other — you say people come to you for teaching, then rate Teaching & People at "read about it" — the contradiction is shown to you rather than averaged away.

The evidence is a checkbox you tick yourself. Nothing is transmitted, inspected or verified. That is the largest gap between what this profile is and what it is for, and pretending otherwise would make the whole exercise worthless. What it currently establishes is that you were willing to state, in a specific domain, that something checkable exists. That is more than a personality quiz asks of you and considerably less than a transcript.

What closes the gap: naming the evidence, so a reader can check it themselves; then assessments marked by someone other than you; then review by people already at a higher tier in that domain. None of those exist yet. When they do, this page will say so and say when.

The tiers are not qualifications

Spark, Kindling, Flame, Torch and Beacon describe knowledge. They are not academic qualifications, they are not awarded by an accredited institution, and they do not entitle anyone to a protected title. Where a tier names a stretch of study — "the working knowledge three to four years of full-time study is meant to produce" — it describes what that study is designed to deliver. It is a statement about the substance, not a claim that you hold anything.

The field rubric

Each of the seventeen fields carries an entry demand vector: the level at which people are typically hired into it, across the same twelve domains, on the same scale as your own ratings. It is capped at 3 — "made real things other people used or paid for" — because no field requires that you already be the person others come to before it will let you in.

These numbers are an editorial judgement, not measured data. They are one person's reading of what these occupations ask for, published as rubric v1 so it can be argued with. The honest version of this is derived from O*NET's free database downloads, joined on SOC codes, with the derivation script published alongside. That has not been done yet.

Fit is one minus a criticality-weighted shortfall: a one-level hole in a domain the field leans on heavily costs proportionally more than the same hole somewhere peripheral. An earlier version weighted every hole equally, with the result that someone who had merely read about all twelve domains outranked a genuine specialist in the field's own core. That was backwards, and fixing it changed most rankings.

The depth score

A cubic power mean over your evidence-adjusted levels, expressed 0–100. The cube is what makes depth beat breadth: one domain you are genuinely good at scores well above twelve you have dabbled in, which is how hiring actually works.

It counts only the levels your evidence supports, so it can never disagree with the tier badges beneath it. It is not a percentile — there is no reference population, and until there are enough real responses to build one, no honest percentile can be quoted. The ± beside it is a real margin, computed by perturbing each rating by its own measurement error and combining the movements in quadrature.

Automation pressure

Shown as one of three bands — high, mixed, low — and deliberately not as a percentage. Nobody has measured what share of any of these occupations current AI systems perform, and printing "72%" would invent precision about the labour market that does not exist. When those figures do exist — O*NET task data joined to something like the Anthropic Economic Index on SOC codes — this becomes a real number with a citation.

What this cannot do

  • It cannot verify anything you told it.
  • It cannot tell you what you are good at if you have not practised anything yet — and it says so instead of guessing.
  • It cannot separate two fields that score within its margin of error, and does not pretend to.
  • It knows nothing about your city, your visa, your savings, your health or your luck, all of which matter more than anything on this page.
  • The ramp figures are rough ranges of what people commonly report, not a forecast, and not a basis for a household budget.
  • It is not careers advice, financial advice, or a substitute for talking to people who actually do the work.

Changing your mind about any of this

The whole rubric, every weight, and the scoring functions are in the public repository, with an invariant test suite that pins the properties described above. If a number here is wrong, it can be shown to be wrong and changed.
